Hawa Computer — a bilingual business platform

A shop, a service desk, an application desk and a training institute in one codebase: a Bangla/English storefront for a Bangladeshi computer business, a customer account area, and a staff admin panel where every action is checked against a permission and written to an audit log.

Overview

Hawa Computer is a computer and IT-services business in Bangladesh. The work it does is not one thing: it sells hardware, fills in government applications for customers who find the forms difficult, runs computer courses, lends books from a small library, and takes payment by bKash, Nagad or Rocket. Each of those is normally its own product with its own admin screen.

This platform puts them behind one account. A customer signs in once and sees their shop orders, service orders, application status, course enrolments, library loans, invoices and support tickets in the same place. Staff sign in to a separate admin panel where the same records are worked, and where the system knows which parts of the business each person is allowed to touch.

Problem and solution

The problem

The business ran on paper, phone calls and a WhatsApp thread. An application's status lived in whoever last spoke to the customer. Payment proofs were screenshots in a chat, reconciled by memory. Nothing could be counted at the end of the month without asking four people, and a customer had no way to check anything without calling during shop hours.

The second problem was language. The people who most need help with a passport or NID application are the least comfortable in an English-only interface, and the staff who process those applications work in Bangla all day. A single-language admin would have been abandoned in a week.

The solution

Model the whole business once, in one schema, and let three interfaces read from it: the public site, the customer account area and the staff panel. Every record — an order, a service order, an application, a payment, a loan — gets an owner, a status and a history, so the answer to "where is this?" is a page, not a phone call.

The rule that shaped the build: every business fact lives in the database, and every change to it goes through a server action that checks a permission first. The UI is a view of that state, never the source of it.

Key features

01

Bilingual by construction

Every page, label, status and validation message has a Bangla and an English form, resolved on the server from a cookie so the first paint is already in the right language. There is no flash of the wrong one, and no client-side dictionary download.

02

Shop, services and applications in one flow

Cart checkout for hardware; service orders with structured detail fields; and long-form government applications with per-service form schemas, document uploads and a tracking ID the customer can follow without signing in.

03

Payments that match how people pay

Manual bKash, Nagad and Rocket transfers: the customer submits a transaction ID and a screenshot, staff verify or reject each one, and the order's payment status, invoice and audit trail follow automatically.

04

A staff panel with real boundaries

Nine staff roles and fifty-four permissions, from viewing an order to refunding a payment to publishing a page. The navigation hides what a role cannot reach, the server action re-checks it, and the write is recorded in the audit log either way.

05

Institute and library, not afterthoughts

Courses with batches and enrolments, attendance, certificates verifiable by number, book loans with due dates and fines, and a downloads library — all attached to the same customer record, all visible in the account area.

Architecture

Next.js 15 with the App Router, TypeScript end to end, Tailwind CSS v4 for styling, Prisma 6 over PostgreSQL, and server actions as the only write path. The app is split into three route groups so the layout, the navigation and the auth gate are decided once per area. 

src/app/
  (site)/         public storefront, services, applications, library, training
  (account)/      customer area: orders, invoices, tickets, files, profile
  (admin)/        staff panel: work queues, catalogue, finance, CMS, settings
src/lib/          auth, permissions, validation, rate-limit, audit, payments, i18n
src/actions/      server actions — the only place that writes
prisma/           schema (48 models), migrations, seed

One schema for the whole business

Forty-eight models and nine enums cover identity and roles, the catalogue, stock movements, orders and payments, service orders and applications with their status history, courses, batches and certificates, books and loans, suppliers and purchases, expenses, CMS content and the audit log. Migrations are committed, so a fresh database is one command away and a deploy is a migration, not a manual step.

Server actions, validated twice

Every mutation is a server action that starts with the same three checks: who is calling, may they do this, and is the input well-formed. Session lookup and the permission check happen on the server against the database, and the payload is parsed with Zod before it reaches Prisma — so a request that skips the form entirely still meets the same wall.

Read models shaped for the page

List screens fetch exactly what they render — order plus items plus customer, or application plus service plus assignee — instead of loading a record and walking relations in the template. On a shared host that is the difference between a page that opens and a page that waits.

Security

The platform handles personal documents — NID numbers, passport details, payment screenshots — so the defaults had to be right before the features were.

  • Ownership checks on every read. A customer's order page filters by the session's user id. Signing in as someone else does not widen the query; it returns 404.
  • Permissions, not roles, at the boundary. Roles are bundles of permissions, and the permission is what the server action checks — so a new role is a data change, not a code change.
  • Passwords with bcrypt, sessions in the database. Session tokens are httpOnly, sameSite and revocable server-side, so signing out actually ends the session.
  • Rate limiting on the doors. Login, registration, contact and payment submission are rate limited, so a script cannot grind passwords or flood the desk.
  • Uploads that are checked, not trusted. Type and size are validated, files are stored outside the web root and served through an authenticated route.
  • Headers and audit. Content Security Policy, HSTS in production, frame and referrer rules — plus an audit entry for every privileged action, with the actor, the target and the time.

Challenges and what I learned

Two languages are a data problem, not a translation problem

The first attempt stored one string per field and swapped it with JavaScript. It broke in three places at once: search results, PDF invoices and the page title. The fix was to treat language as a property of the content — every human-readable field has a Bangla and an English column, the locale is resolved on the server from a cookie, and the document is rendered in the right language the first time.

A missing page is a silent failure

Halfway through, a batch of route files failed to write and the build still succeeded — Next.js is happy to compile a site with holes in it. The only thing that caught it was crawling every internal link and asserting a 200. That crawl is now part of how I verify a release: 145 links, zero 404s, and the same check on the admin panel behind a session.

Manual payments need a paper trail

Mobile-money transfers are real money and they are irreversible. The design had to answer "who said this was paid?" before it answered "how does the customer pay". Every payment carries the payer, the transaction id, the screenshot, the staff member who verified it and the time — and a rejected payment keeps its reason instead of disappearing.

Admin panels are where scope hides

"Just a dashboard" turned into inventory, suppliers, purchases, expenses, refunds, certificates and a CMS. The thing that kept it manageable was refusing to give any of them a special case: a new area is a route group, a permission, a server action and a list view, and it inherits the same guard rails as everything else.
